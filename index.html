Mixed-methods HCI study · Dubai · 2026

The abandoned cart: people build the order, then walk away.

Food delivery apps lose most of their carts, and the industry treats it as a pricing problem. This study asks a different question — at what moment does the decision reverse, and what did the interface do just before it?

Method
Survey + interviews
Analysis

01

The question, and why it is not a pricing question

Abandonment is usually reported as a single number and explained by price. But a person who abandons at the restaurant list has met a different problem from a person who abandons on the payment screen — and only one of those is about money.

I came to this from psychology rather than design. My undergraduate thesis was a quantitative study of rumination and life satisfaction, so my instinct is to measure first: get a distribution, find where it clusters, then go and ask people what was happening at that point.

Research questions

  1. RQ1 — How common is order abandonment, and at which stage does it concentrate?
  2. RQ2 — Which reasons dominate, and do they differ by how often someone orders?
  3. RQ3 — How much weight does delivery time carry relative to price?
  4. RQ4 — What would have to change in the interface for the order to be completed?

03

Interviews — eight people, one question each time: what happened just before you left?

The survey says where. Interviews say why. Each one starts from a real recent abandoned order rather than a hypothetical, because people reconstruct hypotheticals to sound rational.

▸ The interview guide (11 questions)
  1. Tell me about the last time you ordered food through a delivery app.
  2. Have you ever added food to your cart but decided not to order?
  3. At what stage did you decide to leave?
  4. What was the main reason you abandoned the order?
  5. How important is delivery time when deciding whether to order?
  6. How do delivery charges or the final price affect your decision?
  7. Do you compare the same restaurant across different apps?
  8. Have you ever abandoned an order because of minimum-order requirements?
  9. Have payment or checkout issues ever stopped you?
  10. What information would you want to see earlier?
  11. What would make you more likely to complete an order?

06

Five design principles

  1. P1
    Show the true total before the cart is built. The number that reverses the decision is the one revealed last. Estimate it from the first tap.
  2. P2
    Commit to one delivery estimate. A range that narrows unfavourably at checkout destroys the trust that got the user there.
  3. P3
    Surface every gate at the entrance. Minimum order, unavailable payment methods and closing time are conditions of entry, not surprises for checkout.
  4. P4
    Make comparison cheap inside the app. People already compare across apps; the only question is whether you make them leave to do it.
  5. P5
    Treat an abandoned cart as unfinished, not failed. Hold it, and say what changed when they return.

These five became the brief for Plate, the delivery-app concept in the next project, and were then tested in Measuring It, the evaluation study.

07

Limits, stated before anyone asks

  • Self-report measures intention, not behaviour. Asking people why they abandoned a cart gets the reason they can articulate. Log data or a think-aloud with the app open would get the reason they acted on.
  • Convenience sampling. The respondents are reachable through my own networks in Dubai and Pakistan, which skews age, income and app mix. Any percentage here describes this sample, not a population.
  • Single-item measures. Delivery-time importance is one Likert item, so it has no reliability estimate. A multi-item scale would let me report Cronbach's α — which my thesis work did, and this does not.
  • Recall bias. Retrospective accounts of an abandoned order are reconstructions. The interview protocol reduces this by anchoring on the most recent real session, but it does not remove it.
  • Statistical tests are exploratory. With this sample size the χ² and correlation results below are descriptive signposts for design, not confirmatory findings. No p-value here should be read as proof.

What I would do next

Run the same instrument on a stratified sample, add a validated multi-item scale for price sensitivity, and pair it with observed sessions so that stated reasons can be checked against behaviour. Then the funnel below stops being a description and starts being a hypothesis I can test — which is what Measuring It sets out to do.
